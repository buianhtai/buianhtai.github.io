After thinking about software design from first principles — purpose, trade-offs, change, defects, simplicity, testing — it's useful to have everything in one place. This post collects the core laws, rules, and definitions into a reference you can return to. The laws are not independent observations stitched together. They form a chain, and removing any one of them breaks it.

## <Label color="teal">1</Label> The Two Most Important Sentences

If you remember nothing else, remember these:

> **It is more important to reduce the effort of maintenance than it is to reduce the effort of implementation.**
>
> **The effort of maintenance is proportional to the complexity of the system.**

Armed with only these two statements and an understanding of the purpose of software, you could re-derive the entire science of software design — provided you also understood that the complexity of the system comes from the complexity of its individual pieces.

## <Label color="teal">5</Label> The Chain

<Callout color="teal">
The entire body of software design knowledge — patterns, principles, practices — can be derived from two observations: maintenance cost matters more than implementation cost, and maintenance cost is determined by complexity. Everything else follows. Simplify, and you reduce every maintenance cost. Add unnecessary complexity, and you compound every future change.
</Callout>

Software exists to help people (Law 1). The value of a change must exceed its effort, and maintenance effort dominates over time (Law 2). Things will change (Law 3). Changes introduce defects in proportion to their size (Law 4). Simplicity reduces maintenance effort (Law 5). Testing is how you verify behavior after change (Law 6).

Remove any one and the chain breaks. All six are necessary. All six are sufficient.
