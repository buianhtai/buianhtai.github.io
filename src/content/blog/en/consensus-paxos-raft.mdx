Consensus is the problem that unifies everything in distributed systems: how do multiple processes agree on a single value, even when some may fail?

This is not just a theoretical question. It is the question underneath leader election, replication, distributed transactions, and virtually every other strong guarantee in distributed databases. Understand consensus, and you understand why Cassandra, CockroachDB, etcd, and Spanner work the way they do.

## <Label color="teal">1</Label> Consensus: What and Why

A consensus algorithm allows a group of processes to agree on a single value, even if some processes crash. The three properties:

- **Agreement**: no two correct processes decide differently
- **Validity**: the decided value was proposed by some process (not an arbitrary invented value)
- **Termination**: every correct process eventually decides (no infinite waiting)

In distributed databases, consensus is used for:
- **Replicated state machines**: a log of operations is agreed upon by all replicas; applying the log in order produces identical state everywhere (etcd, ZooKeeper, Raft-based systems)
- **Leader election**: choosing a single authoritative leader
- **Distributed commit**: reaching agreement on whether a transaction commits or aborts

FLP impossibility shows that deterministic consensus is impossible in a fully asynchronous system. Practical algorithms circumvent this by assuming partial synchrony (timeouts eventually work) and using randomization or leader election to drive progress.

## <Label color="teal">2</Label> Atomic Broadcast

**Atomic broadcast** (total-order multicast) is closely related to consensus: it ensures all processes deliver the same messages in the same order. Consensus and atomic broadcast are **equivalent** in power — a solution to one gives a solution to the other.

Atomic broadcast properties:
- **Validity**: if a correct process broadcasts a message, all correct processes eventually deliver it
- **Uniform agreement**: if any process delivers a message, all correct processes deliver it
- **Integrity**: no message is delivered more than once
- **Total order**: all processes deliver messages in the same order

**ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast (ZAB)**: the consensus protocol underlying Apache ZooKeeper. Similar to Paxos but with a distinct recovery phase. ZAB operates in two modes: leader-based broadcast (normal operation) and recovery (re-establishing consistency after a leader failure).

## <Label color="teal">3</Label> Paxos

Paxos (Lamport, 1989) is the foundational consensus algorithm. Despite its theoretical importance, it is notoriously difficult to understand and implement correctly.

**Single-decree Paxos** (agreement on a single value) has two phases:

**Phase 1 — Prepare**:
1. A **proposer** picks a proposal number N (must be higher than any previously used)
2. Proposer sends PREPARE(N) to a majority (quorum) of **acceptors**
3. Each acceptor responds with PROMISE(N): "I will not accept any proposal numbered less than N"
4. If an acceptor has already accepted a value with a lower proposal number, it includes that value in the PROMISE response

**Phase 2 — Accept**:
1. If the proposer receives PROMISE responses from a majority:
   - If any acceptor returned a previously-accepted value, the proposer must use that value (preserving prior decisions)
   - Otherwise, the proposer chooses its own proposed value
2. Proposer sends ACCEPT(N, value) to acceptors
3. Acceptors accept if N ≥ their highest-seen proposal number
4. Once a majority accepts, the value is **chosen** (committed)

**Multi-Paxos** extends single-decree Paxos to a log of decisions by optimizing away Phase 1 after a stable leader is established. The leader can skip Phase 1 for subsequent proposals, reducing two round-trips to one.

<Callout color="teal">
Paxos correctness: any two quorums overlap by at least one member. If a value V was chosen in Phase 2 of an earlier round, any subsequent Phase 1 that touches a quorum will see V from the overlapping acceptor and must re-propose V. This prevents two different values from being chosen in different rounds.
</Callout>

**Paxos variants**:
- **Fast Paxos**: clients propose directly to acceptors (bypassing leader for first round), reducing latency by one round-trip in the common case
- **Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos)**: eliminates the single leader bottleneck by allowing any replica to act as leader for non-conflicting commands
- **Flexible Paxos**: generalizes quorum requirements — Phase 1 and Phase 2 quorums don't need to be majorities, as long as every Phase 1 quorum overlaps with every Phase 2 quorum

## <Label color="teal">4</Label> Raft

Raft (Ongaro and Ousterhout, 2014) was designed specifically for **understandability**. Raft decomposes consensus into three relatively independent sub-problems: leader election, log replication, and safety.

**Leader election**:
- Each server maintains a **term** counter (monotonically increasing logical clock)
- If no heartbeat is received from the leader within a random election timeout (150–300ms), a follower becomes a **candidate** and starts an election
- A candidate votes for itself and requests votes from others (RequestVote RPC)
- A server grants a vote if the candidate's term is at least as large as its own, and the candidate's log is at least as up-to-date as the voter's log

**Log replication**:
1. Client sends command to leader
2. Leader appends command to its log (uncommitted)
3. Leader sends AppendEntries RPCs to all followers in parallel
4. Followers append the entry to their local logs and acknowledge
5. When a majority has acknowledged, the leader **commits** the entry
6. Leader notifies followers of the commit on the next AppendEntries; followers commit

**Safety guarantee**: Raft's key safety invariant is the **Leader Completeness Property**: if an entry is committed in term T, it will be present in all leaders elected in subsequent terms. This is enforced by the vote restriction — a candidate cannot be elected unless its log is at least as up-to-date as a majority of voters.

## <Label color="teal">6</Label> Byzantine Consensus

Standard consensus algorithms (Paxos, Raft) assume **crash-recovery faults** — processes fail by stopping. **Byzantine fault tolerance (BFT)** extends this to **arbitrary faults** — processes can send incorrect, contradictory, or malicious messages.

The fundamental result: to tolerate F Byzantine faults, you need at least **3F + 1** total nodes (vs. 2F + 1 for crash faults).

**PBFT (Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, Castro and Liskov, 1999)** was the first practical BFT algorithm. It operates in three phases:
1. **Pre-prepare**: leader sends the request to all replicas
2. **Prepare**: each replica broadcasts that it received the pre-prepare (2F+1 matching prepares = "prepared")
3. **Commit**: each replica broadcasts that it is ready to commit (2F+1 matching commits = "committed-local")

PBFT requires O(N²) message complexity per consensus round — each of N nodes must send messages to all other N nodes. This limits practical use to small replica groups.

**Applications**: PBFT is used in permissioned blockchain systems (Hyperledger Fabric). Permissionless blockchains (Bitcoin, Ethereum) use Nakamoto consensus (proof of work) or proof of stake — different approaches that tolerate a majority of hash power or stake being Byzantine.

## <Label color="teal">7</Label> The Full Picture

The distributed systems topics form a progression:

1. **Fundamentals**: concurrency, fallacies, failure models, FLP impossibility
2. **Failure detection**: how processes detect when peers have failed
3. **Leader election**: designating a coordinator to reduce coordination cost
4. **Consistency models**: the spectrum from linearizability to eventual consistency
5. **Anti-entropy**: mechanisms to repair diverged replicas
6. **Distributed transactions**: atomic commitment across partitions (2PC, 3PC, Spanner, Calvin)
7. **Consensus**: the core problem that ties everything together (Paxos, Raft, PBFT)
