## <Label color="teal">1</Label> The Law of Defect Probability

> **The chance of introducing a defect into your program is proportional to the size of the changes you make to it.**

This is the fourth law in the book. It's derived from observable reality: a one-line change can introduce a bug, but the probability is low. A thousand-line change touches many more assumptions, interactions, and invariants, and the probability of introducing at least one defect is much higher.

The law's practical implication is immediate:

**Make changes as small as possible.**

If you must add a large feature, break it into the smallest independent changes that can each be verified independently. The goal is not to avoid change — the Law of Change tells us change is inevitable. The goal is to pay the defect tax one small installment at a time rather than in one large lump.

<Callout color="amber">
"If it ain't broken, don't fix it" is not the primary message — the point is not to freeze working software, but to be deliberate about the *size* of every change. Large changes are where bugs hide. Small changes are where bugs are visible.
</Callout>

## <Label color="teal">2</Label> Don't Repeat Yourself

The chapter introduces DRY — Don't Repeat Yourself — as a design principle that directly reduces the probability of defects:

> Every piece of information should have a single, authoritative representation in your system.

When logic is duplicated, any change to that logic must be made in every copy. If you miss one copy, you have introduced a defect — not from a new change, but from an incomplete change to something that already existed. DRY defects are some of the most common and some of the most frustrating: the code *looked* updated, but the old behavior persists in a copy the developer forgot existed.

The rule applies beyond code to:
- Configuration (don't state the same constant in two places)
- Documentation (don't maintain the same explanation in three places)
- Schema (don't encode the same business rule in both application and database)

Every duplication is a future site of inconsistency. Every time you change one copy without changing the other, you introduce a defect. DRY is the structural principle that eliminates that entire class of bugs.

## <Label color="teal">3</Label> How These Two Laws Work Together

The Law of Defect Probability and DRY are complementary:

- **Law of Defect Probability** → minimize the *size* of individual changes
- **DRY** → minimize the *number of places* a change must be applied

Together they produce a system where a single logical change requires a small code change in exactly one place. That is the minimum possible defect exposure. When either condition is violated — changes are large, or changes must be applied in multiple places — the defect probability multiplies.

Good design, from this perspective, is design that keeps changes small and keeps the surface area of each change as small as possible. The Law of Simplicity (Chapter 7) will make the mechanism clearer, but the foundation is here: every unnecessary line of code, every duplicated piece of logic, and every unnecessarily large change is a bet that the defect probability law exacts a toll on.
