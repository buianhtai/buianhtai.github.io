<HeroBanner title="Apache Kafka: Architecture & Production Patterns" category="Architecture" icon="logos:kafka" />

Most people describe Kafka as "a message queue." That description causes almost every production incident related to it. Kafka is not a queue — it's a distributed, replicated, append-only commit log. Messages aren't removed when consumed; they're removed on a time or size retention policy, independent of any consumer. That single design choice is why Kafka can replay history, serve hundreds of independent consumer groups from one topic, and survive broker failures without losing acknowledged writes — and it's also why teams that treat it like RabbitMQ hit surprising failure modes.

## <Label color="teal">1</Label> The Core Abstraction: Partitioned, Replicated Log

A **topic** is a named stream. It's split into **partitions** — the actual unit of storage, parallelism, and ordering. Each partition is an ordered, immutable sequence of records, each assigned a monotonically increasing **offset**. Kafka guarantees order *within* a partition only. Across partitions, there is no ordering guarantee — this trade-off is what lets Kafka scale writes and reads horizontally.

Records are never mutated. A "delete" in Kafka is just another record (a tombstone, in compacted topics) or the natural expiry of retention. This immutability is what makes Kafka replayable: a new consumer can start from offset 0 and reconstruct the entire history, or seek to any offset and reprocess from there — something a traditional queue (where consumed messages are gone) cannot do.

<Callout color="amber">
Partition count is a decision you effectively can't reverse cheaply. It sets your maximum consumer parallelism (one partition can only be actively read by one consumer per group) and it's expensive to increase later because existing key-to-partition hashing breaks — messages with the same key can land on a different partition after a resize, breaking per-key ordering.
</Callout>

## <Label color="green">2</Label> Broker, Controller, and Metadata Layer

<ArchLayer badge="STO" badgeColor="rose" label="Storage Layer" items={["Log segments + index files (offset, time)", "Page-cache-backed sequential I/O (zero-copy sendfile)", "Retention: time-based, size-based, or log-compacted"]} />

Kafka's historical dependency on ZooKeeper for controller election and metadata is being phased out via **KRaft** (Kafka Raft), which replaces the ZooKeeper ensemble with a Raft-replicated metadata log inside Kafka itself. This removes an entire external coordination service from the deployment — one less failure domain, one less operational surface to patch and monitor. If you're standing up a new cluster today, KRaft is the default and ZooKeeper mode is deprecated.

The **controller** (a broker elected leader for cluster metadata) is the single source of truth for: which broker leads which partition, which replicas are in-sync, and how to react when a broker dies. Controller failover is itself a Raft leader election — fast, but not instantaneous, and during that window partition leadership changes can stall.

## <Label color="amber">3</Label> Replication and the In-Sync Replica (ISR) Set

Every partition has a **replication factor** (commonly 3). One replica is the leader; the rest are followers that pull from the leader and replicate its log. The **ISR (in-sync replica) set** is the list of replicas that are caught up within a configurable lag threshold (`replica.lag.time.max.ms`). Only ISR members are eligible to become the new leader if the current leader fails.

<Callout color="rose">
The single most common production Kafka incident: someone sets <code>acks=1</code> for producer throughput, the leader broker crashes before the write reaches followers, a new leader is elected from a replica that never saw the message — and the write is gone with no error anywhere in the pipeline. If your data must not be lost, use <code>acks=all</code> with <code>min.insync.replicas &gt;= 2</code> and <code>unclean.leader.election.enable=false</code>. This costs latency, not durability — choose deliberately.
</Callout>

## <Label color="teal">4</Label> Producers, Consumer Groups, and Offset Management

A producer's **partitioner** decides which partition a record lands in — by explicit key hash (same key → same partition → guaranteed per-key order) or round-robin/sticky if no key is given. Batching (`linger.ms`, `batch.size`) trades a small latency window for dramatically higher throughput by grouping records into fewer, larger network requests.

A **consumer group** is Kafka's scaling primitive for reads: every partition is assigned to exactly one consumer within a group, so N partitions support up to N parallel consumers per group. Multiple independent groups can each read the full topic independently — this is how one Kafka topic serves an analytics pipeline, a fraud-detection service, and an audit log simultaneously without them interfering.

They're committed either automatically (`enable.auto.commit=true`, on a timer — risks reprocessing or skipping on crash) or manually after successful processing (safer, "commit after side-effect" pattern). ## <Label color="rose">5</Label> Failure Modes That Actually Happen in Production

<Card>

| Symptom | Root Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumer group stuck rebalancing repeatedly** | `max.poll.interval.ms` exceeded — processing takes longer than Kafka's "is this consumer alive" timeout, so it's kicked from the group mid-batch | Shrink batch size (`max.poll.records`), move slow work off the poll thread, or raise the interval deliberately |
| **Silent data loss after a broker restart** | `acks=1` or `acks=0` combined with unclean leader election allowed | `acks=all`, `min.insync.replicas>=2`, `unclean.leader.election.enable=false` |
| **Producer blocks or throws `TimeoutException` under load** | Partition has no leader (mid-election) or broker is unreachable — buffer fills faster than it drains | Alert on ISR shrinkage and under-replicated partitions *before* this happens; size `buffer.memory` and `max.block.ms` deliberately |
| **Consumer lag grows unbounded** | Consumers can't keep up with producer rate, or a single slow partition (hot key) bottlenecks one consumer while others idle | Scale consumer count to partition count; fix key skew; consider more partitions (irreversible-ish, plan ahead) |
| **Duplicate messages processed downstream** | At-least-once semantics (the Kafka default reality) plus non-idempotent consumer logic | Idempotency key on the consumer side (e.g., upsert by unique event ID), not reliance on "exactly-once" as a silver bullet |
| **"Everything is fine" until disk fills up** | Retention misconfigured (too-long retention, or compaction not enabled on a key-value-style topic) that nobody sized against actual write throughput | Set retention by both time AND size; monitor disk usage per broker, not just cluster aggregate |
| **Split-brain-like duplicate active consumers momentarily** | Consumer group session timeout too aggressive under GC pauses or network blips — a consumer is evicted while still alive, its partitions reassigned, then it comes back | Tune `session.timeout.ms` / heartbeat interval to tolerate realistic GC pause / network jitter for your JVM and environment |

</Card>

<Callout color="amber">
Kafka rarely fails loudly. Most incidents are silent correctness gaps (data loss under `acks=1`, duplicate processing under naive at-least-once) discovered days later during a reconciliation audit — not outages. Treat ISR health, under-replicated-partition count, and consumer lag as first-class alerts, not afterthoughts.
</Callout>

## <Label color="rose">8</Label> Summary

- **It's a log, not a queue.** Retention, not consumption, removes data — this is what enables replay and multiple independent consumer groups.
- **Partitions are the unit of everything** — parallelism, ordering, and the ceiling on consumer scale-out. Choose the count deliberately; it's expensive to change.
- **Replication safety is opt-in.** `acks=1` is fast and silently unsafe; `acks=all` + `min.insync.replicas>=2` + `unclean.leader.election.enable=false` is the durable combination.
- **Delivery semantics are a consumer-side contract**, not just a Kafka setting — commit timing determines at-most-once vs at-least-once, and exactly-once needs both a transactional producer and an idempotent consumer for anything touching external systems.
- **Failures are usually silent correctness gaps**, not outages — monitor ISR health and consumer lag as leading indicators, not just broker uptime.
- **Kafka isn't always the right tool.** A simple task queue with one consumer and no replay requirement is often better served by something with less operational surface — SQS, RabbitMQ. Reach for Kafka when you need replay, multiple independent consumers, or sustained high throughput with ordering guarantees.
