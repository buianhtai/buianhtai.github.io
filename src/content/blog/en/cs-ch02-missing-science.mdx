What separates a field of knowledge from a science? Chapter 2 answers this question precisely, then argues that software design — as currently practiced — fails the test. It has knowledge, it has organization, it even has some rules. But it lacks the most important thing: **laws**. Clearly stated, eternally true principles that let you *predict* what will happen and *derive* other truths. This chapter is the mission statement for the rest of the book.

## <Label color="teal">1</Label> What Software Design Is

The word "design" applies in three ways:

- **As a verb**: planning — deciding on structure, technology, architecture before or while writing
- **As a noun (plan)**: the result of that planning — written documents, diagrams, or decisions held in mind
- **As a noun (description)**: the structure an existing piece of code has or appears to follow

All three apply in software. Code that was never consciously designed still has a design — usually an actively bad one. The chapter defines software design as:

> Anything that involves the architecture of your software system or the technical decisions you make while creating the system.

This deliberately excludes: org structure, team meetings, performance reviews, work schedules. Those are management problems. Software design is strictly the technical decisions about the software itself.

---

## <Label color="teal">2</Label> Every Programmer Is a Designer

One of the most important claims in the book: every programmer on a team is involved in design. The lead architect makes system-level decisions. Senior engineers make subsystem decisions. Junior engineers make file-level and function-level decisions. Even writing a single line of code involves a design decision.

This means nobody on a software team can ignore software design. The problem is that most training focuses on *programming* — syntax, algorithms, tools — not on *design*. The missing science is precisely the body of principles that would guide those decisions at every level.

However, the book is careful to note that design is not a democracy. Design-by-committee produces actively bad results. Each programmer should have authority over their own area, with senior developers having veto power over poor decisions — but the decision must ultimately be made by *an individual*, not by a group vote.

## <Label color="teal">3</Label> What Makes a Science

To have a science, you need:

1. **Collected knowledge** — facts, not opinions, put together somewhere
2. **Organization** — categories, hierarchy, correct relationships between pieces
3. **General truths / basic laws** — not just advice, but provably true statements
4. **Applicability** — it must tell you how to do something in the real world
5. **Verification** — ideally through the scientific method: observe, theorize, experiment, verify

Software design today has 1 and 2. It lacks 3 — and without laws, you can't reliably derive new truths or predict outcomes. Experienced developers *know* the right thing to do, but they usually can't explain *why* it's right in a way that would be convincing to someone else or transferable to new situations.

<Callout color="teal">
The missing piece isn't more advice. Advice is cheap and plentiful. The missing piece is laws — fundamental truths from which correct advice can be derived. Laws let you predict; advice only instructs.
</Callout>

The distinction matters. Rules tell you what to do. Laws tell you *why* — and let you figure out what to do in situations the rules don't cover. The appendix of the book explicitly labels every major statement by category.

When you read something that seems obvious, the test isn't whether you've heard it before. The test is:
- Did you know it was *provably* true?
- Did you know how *important* it was relative to other things?
- Could you *communicate* it clearly to another person?
- Did you understand how it *related* to other knowledge in the field?

A science makes all four of those possible. A collection of opinions does not.
